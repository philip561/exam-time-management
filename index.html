About the project

Large examination halls often run several papers at the same time. Candidates and invigilators have to rely on wall clocks, announcements or times written on a board, which are hard to read from the back of a hall and cannot track several papers of different lengths at once.

This project is an electronic notice board built for that job. A 192 × 32 pixel LED display is divided into three cells, one for each course. An examination officer enters the course names and durations on a keypad and starts the papers separately or together. Each cell then shows the course code in red and the time left in green. A buzzer sounds at 15 minutes and 5 minutes before the end and again at time-up, and the cell then shows TIME UP!

Its main feature is that it keeps correct time through power cuts. Instead of counting down in the microcontroller's memory, it stores each paper's end time and reads the current time from a battery-backed clock, so the remaining time is right the moment power returns. The system is installed in the NLTA Engineering Theatre at ATBU Bauchi for use in examinations held there.

What it does

Shows three papers
Each course gets a cell on the board: its name in red on the top row and its remaining time in green on the bottom row. The text is readable from 25 m.
Takes input from a keypad
An examination officer enters course names and durations on a 4 × 4 keypad, using the multi-tap method of old mobile phones, and starts the countdowns one by one or all together.
Sounds three alerts
A buzzer sounds at 15 minutes remaining, at 5 minutes remaining and at time-up. When a paper ends, its cell shows TIME UP!
Survives power cuts
After a 3 minute outage it shows the correct remaining time at once. The next section explains why.
Built from common parts
Two ATmega328P microcontrollers, a DS1307 real-time clock, twelve P10 LED modules and three 5 V, 20 A switched-mode power supplies.

How it keeps time

Most simple timers count down inside the microcontroller's memory. When the power fails, the count is lost, and the display restarts from a wrong value.

Our system does not count down at all. When a countdown starts, it works out the moment the exam must end and stores that end time. A DS1307 real-time clock with its own battery keeps running when everything else is off. At every moment, the remaining time is simply:

remaining time = end time − current time

Try it in the demo above. Cut the power, wait for it to come back, and compare the two readouts. The display subtracts the stored end time from the clock, so the missing minutes are already accounted for.

How it is built

The units are grouped into four blocks. Keypad and clock go in, two controllers do the work, the display and buzzer give the output, and one power supply feeds everything.

Why two controllers

A 192 × 32 pixel display needs constant refreshing and a lot of frame memory. One ATmega328P would be stretched too thin, so the master handles the keypad, the clock, the buzzer and the top row, while the slave drives the bottom row. They talk over a serial link at 9600 baud, and every message carries a CRC-8 check, so corrupted data is thrown away.

What went wrong along the way

We lost a week to a faulty breadboard. The display only came up when a group member suggested swapping it.

Later, after we thought the system was nearly done, we tested it against a phone. In 30 minutes it had lost 10. That is why the real-time clock is in the design. After we added it, the same kind of test lost 5 seconds in 3 hours.

Once the full board was running, the bottom row began to glitch badly, and sometimes it refused to come up at all. The problem stayed until we replaced the ATmega that drives that row with a new one. After that, the fault was gone.

We laughed at our own mistakes all the way through, and that is a large part of why the team held together.
